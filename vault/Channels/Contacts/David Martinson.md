## Silver Fern combines software automation with virtual ESG support

Silver Fern has two main service lines.

### Configuration aims for end-to-end automation

The team configures software around each customer’s reporting and operating requirements. Work can include partitioning as many as 1,000 data streams for scope two and scope three reporting. It also covers interval-meter data, calculations, emission-factor application, and reporting.

**The goal is to automate the chain from data capture through final reporting.** This usually takes several years. Companies tend to stabilize one portion before adding the next.

### Advisory services fill customer capability gaps

Silver Fern can act as a virtual sustainability team when clients lack capacity or technical knowledge. This includes supporting auditors and advising clients on how to apply greenhouse gas protocol methods to particular data streams.

The firm places high value on people who know:

- how technology can automate sustainability and ESG processes.

David said Silver Fern **could use someone with ESG domain knowledge now**. Medium-term demand is likely to include more business development because that workload is already approaching the limit of his three-day week.

## Mandatory disclosure is expected to lead into delivery against targets

David described Australia as entering another strong period for sustainability. The market had shifted from early sustainability work toward energy management, then returned to sustainability around the year before COVID. He sees Australia as strong now, Europe as a leader, and the US as weaker because ESG has become politically contentious there.

Mandatory disclosure creates an immediate need for companies to build the capability to report. **The next frontier will be progress against the net-zero, energy, and risk commitments recorded in those disclosures.** Reporting establishes a position; reductions and risk treatment require later operational work.

David described this as a maturity process. Major Australian banks, property companies, and other large organizations have pursued substantial sustainability programs over roughly 15 years. Climate risk is newer than energy, carbon, and some social measures. Companies first quantify risks and then determine what to do about them. He expects that progression to take multiple years.

## Physical-risk services may suit Jon’s built-environment experience

Silver Fern has partnered with a risk-assessment company previously used by IBM. The parties are test-driving its software in Australia. It focuses mainly on the financial effects of physical risks.

David said transition risk is harder to model because it depends heavily on each industry and company business model. **The method may vary as widely as the industry sectors themselves.**

Jon sees a possible opening between broad ESG risk reports and detailed engineering responses. Flood, fire, drought, and heat waves directly affect facades and other building elements. A portfolio-level assessment may identify a problem worth tens of millions of dollars, but owners may need more detailed advice on targeted and phased work.

His possible niche would combine:

- **Asset-level fidelity**: Move from a general list of risks to detailed consequences for particular buildings.
- **Engineering responses**: Identify practical interventions instead of only assigning a financial exposure.
- **Phased delivery**: Structure work so owners can address a large problem in manageable stages.
- **Market connection**: Speak with former facade-engineering contacts and identify firms already providing this type of advice.

Jon did not claim that the market has reached this level of detail. Broad ESG assessment may remain sufficient for now, without deep investigation of every listed risk.

## Building risk ratings could follow the Neighbours model

David cited a European scheme called “Creme,” spelled C R E M. An online spreadsheet assesses alignment with net-zero or climate-scenario targets. Invisi had built a report for European customers that used the approach to identify assets or buildings that could become unattractive to investors under those scenarios.

David suggested that Australian property companies could follow a path similar to Neighbours ratings. No rule requires a five- or six-star building, yet property companies compete over portfolio averages such as 5.1. **A comparable building-risk score could give investors a clear way to distinguish resilient assets from those likely to perform poorly under specific climate scenarios.**

Jon expected Green Star to seek a role if such a niche emerges. He also noted the contrast in Australia between a strong least-cost commercial culture and frameworks such as Neighbours and ASRS, which he regards as world-leading. David added Angers as an earlier example and said ASRS stands alongside leading European sustainability frameworks.
