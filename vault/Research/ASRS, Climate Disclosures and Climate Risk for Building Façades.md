# ASRS, Climate Disclosures and Climate Risk for Building Façades

## Purpose

This document summarises the discussion on Australia’s sustainability reporting requirements and their practical relevance to climate risk in **building façades**, covering both **new construction** and **remedial/rectification works**.

The central conclusion is that climate-resilient façade design, maintenance and remediation are no longer only technical or facilities-management matters. For entities within scope of Australian Sustainability Reporting Standards (ASRS), façade-related climate risks can become financial-reporting matters where they could reasonably affect cash flows, asset values, insurance, access to finance, cost of capital, operating costs or capital expenditure.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Australia’s mandatory climate-related financial reporting regime commenced on **1 January 2025** and is being introduced in phases.
- The core mandatory standard is **AASB S2 Climate-related Disclosures**.
- AASB S2 requires in-scope entities to disclose climate-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect:
  - Cash flows
  - Financial position
  - Financial performance
  - Access to finance
  - Cost of capital
- Façades are highly relevant because they sit at the interface between the building and climate hazards, including heat, wind, rain, storms, bushfire exposure, salt, humidity and hail.
- Façade risks can drive material costs through water ingress, defects, premature deterioration, insurance claims, tenant disruption, higher cooling loads, remediation capex, litigation, reduced asset value and building obsolescence.
- New façade works should consider future climate exposure, not only historic climate data or minimum code compliance.
- Remedial works should investigate root causes and future climate exposure rather than simply performing “like-for-like” repairs.
- Climate-related façade risks should be integrated into governance, strategy, enterprise risk management, capital planning, insurance, procurement and asset-management systems.
- Strong technical records—such as condition reports, testing results, design assumptions, maintenance histories and remediation options—can become important evidence for ASRS disclosures and assurance.

## 1. Australian Climate Reporting Context

### ASRS and AASB S2

Australia’s mandatory climate-related financial reporting framework applies to specified large entities and financial institutions. It is phased in over time.

| Group | First financial years beginning on or after | General relevance |
|---|---:|---|
| Group 1 | 1 January 2025 | Largest listed and unlisted entities, financial institutions and certain NGER-reporting entities |
| Group 2 | 1 July 2026 | Additional large entities entering the mandatory regime |
| Group 3 | 1 July 2027 | Further entities entering scope, subject to legislative thresholds and conditions |

The main climate-reporting standard is **AASB S2 Climate-related Disclosures**.

AASB S2 requires disclosures about climate-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect an entity’s prospects. In practice, the question is whether climate issues could affect:

- Cash flows
- Financial performance
- Financial position
- Asset values
- Insurance availability or cost
- Access to finance
- Cost of capital
- Capital expenditure
- Revenue and operating costs

### Four AASB S2 pillars

AASB S2 uses four core disclosure areas:

1. **Governance**
2. **Strategy**
3. **Risk management**
4. **Metrics and targets**

For façade-related issues, reporting should go beyond generic statements such as “climate change is a risk.” It should identify the specific risk, explain exposure and vulnerability, show how management responds, and identify material financial effects where applicable.

## 2. Why Building Façades Matter

Façades are a critical climate-risk interface because they protect buildings from external conditions while strongly influencing energy use, indoor comfort, maintenance demands and asset durability.

They can affect:

- Water penetration and moisture damage
- Wind resistance and structural safety
- Fire and bushfire exposure
- Solar heat gain and cooling demand
- Thermal performance and occupant comfort
- Corrosion and material durability
- Insurance claims and policy conditions
- Building availability and tenant disruption
- Long-term capital expenditure
- Asset value and marketability

Australia has already experienced significant warming, increasing extreme heat, increased intensity in certain heavy rainfall events, and heightened fire-weather exposure in many regions. These shifts have direct implications for façade design, inspection, maintenance and remediation.

## 3. Main Physical Climate Risks for Façades

| Climate hazard | Façade risk pathway | Potential financial impact |
|---|---|---|
| Extreme rainfall | Water ingress through joints, sealants, flashings, glazing, drainage paths, penetrations and interfaces | Emergency works, internal damage, claims, tenant disruption, legal exposure, increased capex |
| Wind-driven rain | Water forced through external-wall systems under pressure | Recurrent leaks, premature deterioration, remedial costs, operational disruption |
| High winds, storms and cyclones | Glazing breakage, cladding movement or detachment, fixing failures, debris hazards | Safety incidents, replacement cost, insurance claims, business interruption |
| Heatwaves | Thermal expansion, sealant and gasket ageing, coating degradation, higher solar heat gain | Higher cooling costs, thermal discomfort, maintenance and early replacement |
| UV exposure | Degradation of sealants, membranes, coatings and polymer components | Shorter service life, increased inspection and renewal requirements |
| Bushfire and smoke | Ember attack, radiant heat, smoke ingress, deterioration of seals and external-wall components | Upgrade costs, disruption, insurance risk, safety and compliance exposure |
| Coastal exposure | Salt spray, corrosion, humidity, wetting and drying cycles | Fixing corrosion, coating degradation, accelerated maintenance and replacement |
| Hail | Damage to glass, skylights, louvres, panels and adjacent systems | Claims, replacement works, operational downtime |
| Flooding and inundation | Damage to lower façade zones, interfaces, services penetrations and materials | Repair costs, building downtime, asset impairment |
| Compound events | Multiple hazards occurring together or repeatedly | Cumulative damage, unexpected capital expenditure, increased risk of system failure |

## 4. Climate Risk in New Façade Works

### Design implications

For new construction, climate risk should be considered during concept design, detailed design, procurement, construction, commissioning and handover.

Important design considerations include:

- Future heat, rainfall, wind, bushfire and coastal exposure
- Wind-driven rain resistance and pressure equalisation
- Drainage, flashing, sill, end-dam and penetration detailing
- Façade movement, expansion and contraction allowances
- Sealant and gasket durability
- Thermal performance and solar heat gain
- Insulation continuity and thermal bridging
- Airtightness and condensation control
- Glazing type, shading and orientation
- Corrosion protection and material compatibility
- Fire performance and bushfire-related exposure
- Access for inspection, maintenance, resealing, cleaning and replacement
- Ability to adapt the façade in future

### Design for future climate, not historic climate alone

A façade may comply with a historic design basis while still becoming vulnerable over its intended service life. A resilient design should consider whether:

- Rainfall intensity may exceed assumptions used in detailing and testing.
- Higher temperatures may increase material movement or degradation.
- Wind and storm exposure may affect glazing, cladding, fixings and interfaces.
- More frequent extreme heat may increase cooling demand and occupant discomfort.
- Future bushfire or coastal exposure may alter material performance requirements.
- Maintenance intervals should be shortened because of more severe exposure.

### Procurement and construction risks

Climate risk also affects construction delivery.

Key areas include:

- Material availability and price volatility.
- Dependence on imported aluminium, glass, steel, sealants, membranes and insulation.
- Ability of suppliers to provide environmental product declarations and carbon data.
- Weather-related delays and wet-weather quality risks.
- Temporary works exposure prior to façade completion.
- Defect risk arising from rushed installation after weather delays.
- Traceability of façade materials and components.
- Quality assurance, mock-ups, testing and hold points.
- Availability of qualified façade installers and specialist subcontractors.

### Transition risks in new works

Transition risks arise from the movement toward lower-emission, more energy-efficient and more resilient buildings.

Examples include:

- More demanding operational-energy expectations.
- Market preference for high-performance buildings.
- Requirements from lenders, funds, government procurement or tenants.
- Embodied-carbon reporting requirements.
- Client expectations for environmental product declarations.
- Greater scrutiny of carbon-intensive materials.
- Insurance requirements for resilient construction.
- Exposure to greenwashing claims if “sustainable” or “climate-resilient” claims are not substantiated.

## 5. Climate Risk in Remedial Façade Works

### The fundamental shift

Traditional remedial work asks:

> What failed, and how can it be repaired?

Climate-resilient remedial work asks:

> What failed, why did it fail, how may future climate conditions affect it, and will the repair remain suitable through the relevant asset life?

This distinction is important because a repair that restores current appearance or functionality may not address the underlying failure mechanism.

### Common remedial risk areas

| Issue | Climate relevance | Preferred response |
|---|---|---|
| Recurrent leaks | May indicate inadequate detailing under severe rainfall or wind-driven rain | Investigate complete water path and remedy root causes |
| Failed sealants | May reflect excessive movement, UV degradation, poor joint geometry or incompatible materials | Assess movement, exposure, adhesion, durability and maintenance access |
| Corroded fixings | May result from salt, moisture retention, incompatible materials or insufficient protection | Assess corrosion mechanisms and revise materials/detailing |
| Thermal discomfort | Can increase cooling costs and reduce tenant satisfaction | Assess glazing, shading, insulation, airtightness and thermal bridges |
| Condensation and mould | May worsen with poorly coordinated air, vapour and thermal-control layers | Review building physics, ventilation and envelope interfaces |
| Deteriorating cladding | May indicate shortened life caused by moisture, heat, UV or environmental exposure | Prepare condition baseline and life-cycle intervention plan |
| Fire or bushfire vulnerability | May become more significant with increased fire-weather exposure | Integrate relevant fire, façade and bushfire assessment |
| Lack of access | Prevents routine inspection and maintenance, causing deferred deterioration | Include permanent access, safe replacement and inspection strategies |
| Major façade replacement | Creates high capex, waste and embodied-carbon exposure | Compare repair, partial replacement and full replacement options |

### Avoid maladaptation

A remedial solution can unintentionally create new climate or building-performance problems.

Examples include:

- Applying a darker finish that increases heat absorption and thermal movement.
- Increasing airtightness without addressing ventilation and condensation.
- Overcladding without resolving concealed moisture, weight, fire or maintenance issues.
- Replacing glazing with a lower-cost option that worsens solar heat gain or energy performance.
- Selecting low-upfront-carbon materials that have poor durability and require early replacement.

A good remedial strategy documents trade-offs between:

- Safety
- Compliance
- Weatherproofing
- Fire performance
- Climate resilience
- Operational energy
- Embodied carbon
- Lifecycle cost
- Maintenance access
- Occupant disruption
- Insurance
- Asset value

## 6. ASRS Disclosure Implications for Façade Risk

### Governance

Entities should identify who is accountable for climate-related façade risk.

Relevant questions include:

- Does the board receive information about façade condition, climate exposure and major remediation?
- Who owns façade-related climate risk within the organisation?
- Is responsibility allocated across asset management, engineering, facilities, sustainability, procurement, insurance and executive leadership?
- Are material façade risks escalated to the board or risk committee?
- Are climate risks included in development approval, capital expenditure and acquisition decisions?
- Are external consultants, contractors and certifiers engaged with clear responsibilities?

### Strategy

Entities should identify where façade-related climate risks and opportunities affect their business model.

For property owners and asset managers, this may include:

- Directly owned buildings
- Development pipeline
- Assets under management
- Tenant comfort and business continuity
- Capital planning
- Asset acquisitions and divestments
- Insurance coverage and claims
- Lending and valuation exposure
- Long-term asset obsolescence

For façade contractors, builders and manufacturers, this may include:

- Site weather risk
- Supply-chain dependence
- Product durability and warranty risk
- Defects and professional indemnity exposure
- Carbon-intensive materials
- Demand for energy upgrades and climate-resilient remediation
- Ability to provide testing, traceability and lifecycle performance data

### Risk management

A practical façade climate-risk process should include:

1. Create a portfolio and façade register.
2. Identify façade types, ages, materials, conditions and known defects.
3. Screen each asset for relevant climate hazards.
4. Assess vulnerability of façade systems and critical interfaces.
5. Review previous leaks, claims, repairs, failures and maintenance records.
6. Estimate potential financial exposure.
7. Prioritise action based on safety, condition, exposure, criticality and financial materiality.
8. Establish mitigation, maintenance and capital-work plans.
9. Monitor outcomes after storms, extreme heat, repairs and major works.
10. Update risk assessments when new climate information, claims, acquisitions or material events arise.

### Metrics and targets

Possible façade-related metrics include:

| Category | Example measures |
|---|---|
| Condition | Percentage of façade area inspected; number of high-risk defects; remaining useful life |
| Water performance | Water-ingress incidents; weatherproofing test outcomes; repeat leak rate |
| Resilience | Assets screened for climate hazards; high-risk assets with funded adaptation plans |
| Financial | Forecast and actual façade capex; claims cost; insurance premium and excess changes |
| Energy | Cooling-energy intensity; peak cooling demand; overheating complaints |
| Carbon | Embodied carbon of façade works; recycled content; waste diversion; supplier EPD coverage |
| Delivery quality | Mock-up/test completion; defect rate; traceability coverage; warranty claims |
| Targets | Reduction in high-risk façade exposure; inspection completion rates; remediation programme milestones |

## 7. Scenario Analysis for Façades

Scenario analysis should test how façade systems, portfolios and business models perform under different plausible climate conditions.

It is not intended to predict one precise future. Its purpose is to identify vulnerabilities, quantify possible effects and support better decisions.

### Practical scenario-analysis steps

| Step | Key question |
|---|---|
| Define scope | Is the assessment for one building, a portfolio, a project pipeline or a product line? |
| Define time horizons | What do short, medium and long term mean for the organisation and the asset life? |
| Identify hazards | Which hazards are relevant for the location and asset? |
| Assess vulnerability | Which materials, details, interfaces and systems are exposed? |
| Assess consequences | What repairs, downtime, claims, energy costs or asset-value effects may occur? |
| Quantify financial effects | What changes in capex, opex, insurance, revenue, valuation or financing may arise? |
| Identify adaptation options | What repair, upgrade, replacement, maintenance or procurement changes are available? |
| Document assumptions | What climate data, scenarios, limits and uncertainties were used? |

### Example

A commercial property portfolio experiences recurring water ingress in older curtain-wall buildings.

A structured climate-risk response could include:

- Reviewing rainfall and wind-driven-rain exposure.
- Investigating drainage paths, interfaces, flashings, glazing systems and seals.
- Reviewing previous claims and tenant disruptions.
- Estimating costs of patch repairs, targeted remediation and staged renewal.
- Assessing implications for insurance, tenant retention, operating costs and asset value.
- Funding a multi-year remediation programme.
- Monitoring post-remediation performance and repeat failures.

Where the potential financial effects are material, the issue may become relevant to AASB S2 disclosures.

## 8. Financial Reporting Connections

Façade climate risks may need to be considered alongside conventional financial reporting issues.

Potential financial-statement connections include:

- Asset impairment and recoverable amount assessments.
- Property valuations and fair-value assumptions.
- Useful lives and depreciation of façade components.
- Capital expenditure forecasts.
- Maintenance versus replacement classification.
- Provisions for remediation, defects, warranties or legal claims.
- Contingent liabilities.
- Insurance premiums, excesses, exclusions and uninsured exposures.
- Revenue and margin impacts from construction delays or defects.
- Tenant disruption, rent abatements and vacancy risk.
- Financing terms, lender requirements and cost of capital.
- Cash-flow impacts from energy use, repairs, maintenance and major works.

The key test remains whether the climate-related risk or opportunity could reasonably be expected to affect the entity’s prospects.

## 9. Embodied Carbon and Scope 3 Relevance

Façades can have significant embodied-carbon impacts due to use of:

- Aluminium
- Glass
- Steel
- Concrete
- Insulation
- Membranes
- Coatings
- Sealants
- Fixings
- Access equipment
- Transport
- Demolition and waste disposal

For new works and remediation, entities should consider:

- Repairing rather than replacing where technically appropriate.
- Partial replacement rather than complete replacement.
- Reuse and recycling potential.
- Recycled content.
- Environmental product declarations.
- Durability and expected service life.
- Whole-of-life carbon.
- Operational-energy benefits.
- Waste generated during remediation.
- Supply-chain data quality.
- Carbon effects associated with construction and disposal.

The preferred approach is generally whole-of-life assessment rather than focusing only on lowest upfront cost or lowest manufacturing carbon.

## 10. Actionable Guidance

### For property owners and asset managers

- Establish a façade asset register with location, type, age, condition, defect history and replacement value.
- Screen buildings for climate hazards such as heat, rainfall, wind, bushfire, coastal exposure and flooding.
- Prioritise buildings with repeated leaks, insurance claims, safety issues, poor thermal performance or significant planned capex.
- Integrate climate risk into acquisition due diligence, valuations, maintenance planning and capital approvals.
- Require consultants to address climate exposure, durability, maintenance access and adaptation options.
- Connect façade risk to insurance renewal and enterprise risk management.
- Build auditable records for ASRS reporting and future assurance.

### For developers, builders and façade contractors

- Include climate risk in tender reviews and project risk registers.
- Design and specify for future climate exposure where relevant.
- Use clear performance requirements for weatherproofing, wind, thermal performance, durability, fire and maintenance.
- Maintain robust mock-up, testing and hold-point processes.
- Improve product and material traceability.
- Retain as-built records, test reports, warranties and maintenance requirements.
- Obtain supplier carbon and environmental data where material.
- Avoid unsupported sustainability, resilience or net-zero claims.

### For remedial façade specialists

- Use a root-cause investigation process rather than symptom-based patching.
- Assess future climate exposure as part of condition investigations.
- Offer clients option appraisals comparing repair, partial replacement and full replacement.
- Compare options using safety, compliance, resilience, capex, lifecycle cost, carbon and disruption.
- Include post-remediation monitoring, inspection and testing in scopes.
- Preserve technical evidence for warranty, insurance, litigation defence and owner reporting.
- Explain residual risk clearly where full risk elimination is not feasible.

## 11. Recommended Project Record

For every substantial new façade project or remedial façade project, maintain a “climate and façade risk file” containing:

- Building and site description.
- Façade typology, materials, height and exposure.
- Asset value and expected service-life assumptions.
- Historic defect, maintenance, leak and claims records.
- Hazard screening and climate-data sources.
- Relevant time horizons.
- Condition assessment and root-cause findings.
- Water-path and interface analysis.
- Design assumptions and technical criteria.
- Test plans, mock-ups and performance results.
- Material schedules and traceability records.
- Durability, thermal, fire and maintenance considerations.
- Options analysis.
- Lifecycle-cost assessment.
- Embodied-carbon or environmental-product data where relevant.
- Procurement and supply-chain risk assessment.
- Insurance and warranty considerations.
- Capital expenditure plan.
- Residual risks and responsible owners.
- Monitoring and maintenance requirements.
- Linkage to enterprise risk registers and ASRS materiality assessment.

## 12. Important Cautions

- ASRS is a financial-disclosure framework. It does not replace technical façade design, engineering, construction, certification or compliance obligations.
- Technical compliance still depends on the National Construction Code, relevant Australian Standards, state and territory legislation, planning requirements, contract documents and competent professional advice.
- Not every façade defect is material for ASRS purposes.
- Materiality depends on the likely effect of the climate-related issue on the reporting entity’s prospects.
- Climate exposure is highly location-specific.
- Climate scenarios contain uncertainty; disclosures should be transparent about assumptions, limitations and methodologies.
- Assurance requirements will increase over time, so organisations should design systems and evidence trails with auditability in mind.
- Environmental and resilience claims should be specific, evidence-based and appropriately qualified.

---

## Conclusion

Climate change increases the importance of façade performance because façades control how buildings respond to rain, wind, heat, fire exposure, moisture, corrosion and changing environmental conditions.

For in-scope Australian entities, façade-related climate risks may become material ASRS matters where they affect building condition, repair costs, energy demand, insurance, tenant disruption, asset value, capital expenditure, financing or long-term business prospects.

The most effective response is to combine:

- Strong façade engineering
- Site-specific climate-risk assessment
- Condition monitoring
- Root-cause remediation
- Lifecycle-cost analysis
- Whole-life carbon consideration
- Robust governance
- Financial planning
- Traceable project records
- Auditable risk-management processes

The practical aim is not simply to repair or construct façades that meet immediate requirements. It is to create and maintain façade systems that remain safe, weatherproof, durable, efficient, maintainable and financially resilient over their intended life.
