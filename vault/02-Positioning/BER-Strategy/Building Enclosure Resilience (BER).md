# Building Enclosure Resilience (BER)

## A New Service Group for JFS Engineers — Offering, Market Case & 3-Year Roadmap

_A naming note: "Enclosure" is used for the group and practice name — it reads more precisely than "façade" (naturally covers roof and envelope assemblies too) and is more distinctive as a brand. JFS's own service list still uses "façade" throughout, and that terminology is kept intact wherever this document quotes or maps to an existing service._

## 1. Why Now

Four forces are converging on the building envelope at once:

- **Regulatory**: Mandatory climate-related financial disclosure (AASB S2/ASRS) is already in force for large entities (Group 1, since Jan 2025), and expands to more businesses in July 2026 (Group 2) and July 2027 (Group 3). Façades are one of the most exposed, legible parts of a climate-disclosure story — water ingress, wind/storm damage, thermal underperformance, corrosion, bushfire exposure.
- **Financial/Insurance**: Disclosure alone doesn't satisfy lenders or insurers. Debt covenants increasingly reference physical climate resilience, and insurers are starting to price — or decline — cover based on it. This turns the conversation from "disclose your risk" into a sharper, more urgent one: is this asset insurable, financeable, and lettable at all. It's the sharpest commercial hook of the four forces here, though it needs careful handling — see Section 7.
- **Technical**: Tier-1 firms (Arup, Buro Happold, Inhabit, AECOM) compete on deep physics-based analysis — thermal, hygrothermal, wind/CFD, daylight, structural movement, testing. That's the credibility bar for "market leader," but it's a service list, not a moat — most of it is buildable by a focused mid-size firm.
- **Operational**: The tooling to deliver that analysis at scale — canonical simulation pipelines, human-in-loop offshore production — now exists outside the big firms' walls, letting a lean team compete without Arup-scale headcount.

JFS already has real technical depth in place: System Thermal Assessment, Condensation Risk Assessment, Cladding Pressure Assessment, CFD Wind Assessment, and Overall Building Energy Modeling all appear on JFS's current service list. The opportunity isn't building physics capability from nothing — it's organising that capability into a named, coherent service group; upgrading select methodologies from code-compliance-grade to resilience-grade; and connecting the output to the commercial drivers above.

**The clearest evidence for this gap**: a review of climate-related disclosures across 33 major Australian and global real estate groups found that none of them — not one — cite AS 1170.2 (the Australian wind-load design standard) or a Bushfire Attack Level rating, despite the large majority disclosing wind/cyclone and bushfire exposure at the portfolio level. Facade-level condition data is essentially unreported across the cohort too — only one group (Abacus Group) discloses a portfolio-wide facade inspection programme. Asset owners are naming the hazards; almost none are grounding the response in an actual engineering standard. That's the gap this whole positioning is built on, evidenced rather than assumed.

**The position to claim**: JFS establishes Building Enclosure Resilience (BER) as the mid-market specialist connecting façade engineering, physics-based building performance analysis, and climate-risk advisory into one service — faster and leaner than the multidisciplinary giants, more technically rigorous than compliance-only competitors. Against Tier-1 firms specifically, the durable counter-position is agility, senior-level access, proportionate fixed fees, and independent technical assurance — not matching their headcount or breadth.

## 2. The Service Architecture

This is the core of the offering: four tiers, each with a distinct commercial purpose, deliberately kept separate so none gets diluted by the others.

### 2.1 Core Resilience Analysis — the technical centre of gravity

Three hazard-driven capabilities, built and sold together — a client exposed to one hazard usually has exposure to the others, so a joint offer sells better than a single-issue one:

|Capability|What it answers|Methodology|
|:--|:--|:--|
|Thermal bridging & condensation|Will the envelope avoid surface and interstitial moisture failure over its life?|Steady-state (THERM-class) now; dynamic hygrothermal (WUFI-class) as the resilience-grade upgrade|
|Wind & water penetration|Will the façade withstand intensifying storm, hail, and wind-driven rain?|Cladding pressure assessment + CFD wind assessment (existing); physical wind-tunnel commissioning for tall/complex buildings (a gap); glazing/cladding hail-impact resistance|
|Overheating & thermal performance|Will occupants and the building perform under peak and future heat?|Design-day, heatwave, and future-climate weather files — deliberately not annual consumption|

This tier maps directly to the physical hazards named in the ASRS/AASB S2 material. It's genuinely resilience work, not general design-quality service — which is why the deprioritised list in 2.4 stays deprioritised even though it's technically adjacent. Hail is worth calling out specifically: across the 33-group disclosure review above, it's the most _financially_ quantified peril found — Scentre Group puts hailstorm vulnerability at 12.8% of portfolio book value (~$4.3bn), ahead of both bushfire (4.5%) and flooding (2.1%) — while Dexus discloses that it hasn't modelled hail at all "due to data uncertainty." That combination (material, quantified by at least one owner, but unmodelled by others) is close to an ideal wedge for a specialist technical offer.

Worth flagging now, expanded in 2.3: the overheating/peak-load methodology here isn't only a physical-hazard tool. It's also the direct diagnostic for one specific transition-risk scenario — an under-performing envelope becoming the bottleneck when an owner electrifies plant (gas to heat-pump), since heat pumps deliver less thermal output per hour than gas and peak loads can exceed the new system's capacity. Same capability, two different commercial reasons to buy it.

### 2.2 New-Build Lead-In: The Pre-Planning Advisory Brief

The original framing here treated JV3, DA daylight compliance, and NSW materials-index reporting as the lead-in itself — sold directly, pre-DA, to get JFS onto the project. That's weaker than it looks: ESD consultants already hold the DA front door and won't surrender the Sustainability Management Plan, JV3 modelling, or daylight calculations, and clients resist paying for a second pre-permit consultant to duplicate that scope. Competing with ESD consultants on lightweight compliance is a losing battle, not a lead-in — worth correcting rather than defending.

**The actual lead-in mechanism**: a **Pre-Planning Advisory Brief**, given directly to the architect before an ESD consultant is even appointed — a near-zero-cost, automated loss-leader built from JFS archetype databases and parametric screening tools, not a billable pre-DA consultancy engagement. Two parts:

- **What the architect actually wants at concept stage** — directional massing and procurement guidance, not a compliance manifesto: a governing-standards strip (NCC Section J, Green Star, NABERS, WELL — confirm the current NCC edition before this is client-facing, Section 8); glazing spec (minimum VLT, tint guidance); system-suite guidance (standard thermally-broken dies vs. bespoke extrusions, with lead-time warnings); a budget-uplift index; and a massing-bounds table by elevation (target WWR%, feasible U-value/SHGC, shading/spandrel/fin rules).
- **A "forward resilience radar"** — four flagged, unresolved risks the annual-compliance view doesn't capture: peak heatwave overheating (42°C design-day sizing vs. annual averages — this is 2.1's overheating methodology, not JV3's), extreme rain and box-gutter capacity, hail and cladding wind-suction, and embodied carbon in extrusions. These aren't answered in the Brief — they're deliberately planted as the scope that gets resolved, and paid for, in Detailed Design.

**The commercial mechanics**: JFS runs this internally as an automated parametric screen ("ghost JV3") at near-zero production cost. By the time an ESD consultant is appointed, weeks or months later, the architect already has JFS's numbers and pushes the ESD consultant to match them rather than starting from scratch — which relegates the ESD consultant to a compliance box-ticking role while JFS owns the underlying design logic. JV3, DA daylight, and materials-index reporting still happen — just downstream, resolved through Detailed Design (often via the Core three, 2.1) rather than as JFS's own billable pre-DA front door.

**The methodology distinction that made the old framing wrong is still worth keeping explicit**, since it now explains _why_ the radar items are separate from what the ESD consultant delivers: JV3 uses standard/reference-year weather files to answer an annual-compliance question; Core overheating (2.1) uses design-day/heatwave/future files to answer a survival question — same software, different methodology. Daylight splits the same way (ADG-type DA compliance vs. daylight/glare comfort, deprioritised in 2.4), and embodied carbon splits the same way too (materials-index compliance vs. the broader advisory work in 2.3).

**Liability posture**: framed strictly as non-statutory directional design advice — stated on the document itself that it "does not replace statutory JV3." This is a fourth item for the reliance-language discipline in Section 7, not a special case.

This reframes what 2.2 is for: not a direct lead-in sale, but a trust-building loss-leader that wins the relationship before the paid work is even scoped.

### 2.3 Building Enclosure Resilience Audits — existing-stock retrofit & assurance

Two different triggers lead an owner to essentially the same staged audit product — worth keeping them distinct rather than collapsing into one generic "audit," since they emphasise different technical content:

- **Transition-risk / CRREM-linked**: ESG consultants and sustainability advisors run CRREM (Carbon Risk Real Estate Monitor) transition-risk assessments that flag when an asset may "strand" against a decarbonisation pathway — but they can't say whether the façade is the cause, what's technically achievable, or what it costs. Sold via partnership with the ESG advisor rather than direct to the owner. Capex/carbon-weighted.
- **Physical-risk / insurance & debt-covenant-linked**: debt covenants increasingly reference physical climate resilience, and insurers are starting to price cover based on it. An owner needs assurance-ready engineering evidence that the physical fabric will hold up. Sold via insurers, institutional lenders, Big 4 climate-advisory practices, or direct to asset managers. Physical-hazard/structural-weighted — this is where the Core Resilience Analysis capability (2.1) gets sold directly into existing buildings, not just new ones.

**The diagnostic Level 1 actually needs to make**: a lot of what gets labelled "climate transition risk" in ESG reporting is routine end-of-life refurbishment mislabelled — and conflating the two obscures where the real financial exposure sits. This is the substance of a Level 1 screen, not just its packaging:

||Routine code compliance (not transition risk)|True climate transition risk|
|:--|:--|:--|
|Trigger|Owner-initiated end-of-life refurbishment|External policy/market/technology shift arriving _ahead_ of planned end-of-life|
|Timing|Planned lifecycle capex, budgeted years in advance|Accelerated, forced capex, premature capital reallocation|
|Financial impact|Standard asset enhancement to maintain market parity|Asset stranding, rental discounting, or lease bans if left unaddressed|

Four scenarios actually generate the second column — a Level 1 screen should test for these specifically, not produce a generic "climate risk" flag:

1. **Premature regulatory stranding** — legislation penalising existing fabric before its physical design life expires. UK MEES (mandatory EPC B by 2030) and France's DPE marketing bans are the clearest overseas examples; Australia doesn't yet have a direct equivalent for existing-building envelopes specifically, worth tracking as state-based minimum standards evolve (Section 8).
2. **The electrification bottleneck** — covered in 2.1: a leaky or under-insulated envelope becomes the literal capacity bottleneck when gas plant is replaced with heat pumps.
3. **Scope 3 tenant flight** — tenants bound by their own decarbonisation targets (SBTi, ASRS) discounting or rejecting leases in poor-envelope assets. The exposure here is unplanned vacancy and cap-rate expansion, not energy cost.
4. **Embodied-vs-operational carbon conflict** — upgrading a curtain wall to cut operational emissions can itself breach a corporate embodied-carbon cap (real examples: British Land's 500 kgCO2e/m² limit, BXP's 14% intensity-reduction target). This sharpens the carbon distinction already drawn in 2.2 — the operational/embodied trade-off isn't a footnote here, it can be the transition risk itself. Facade retention/adaptive reuse is emerging as the standard mitigation major owners actually use rather than full replacement — Landsec cut upfront embodied carbon 60% through structural retention, Dexus's Brisbane adaptive reuse avoided ~20,800 tCO2e by retaining "existing structure, lift core and façade" — worth building as a standard option (alongside "do nothing," "targeted intervention," and "full replacement") in every Retrofit Options & Carbon-Capex Plan, not an edge case.

This is a genuinely sharp commercial differentiator: not "we help with energy efficiency," but "we tell you whether this is routine maintenance or a real transition-risk exposure, before you commit capital to the wrong one."

Both funnel into the same staged product: a **3-Tier Building Enclosure Resilience Audit**, adapted from the AS/NZS 3598 energy-audit framework — a structure lenders, insurers, and auditors already recognise, which shortens the sales conversation considerably.

|Tier|Scope|Maps to|
|:--|:--|:--|
|**Level 1 — Desktop Screening**|Macro climate-hazard mapping against façade design codes and known building data, applying the routine-vs-transition-risk diagnostic above|A fixed-fee, low-cost entry point regardless of which trigger brought the client in|
|**Level 2 — Physical & Thermal Audit**|Non-destructive site inspection, thermal bridging analysis, seal-degradation timelines, phased capex remediation plan (±15–20% accuracy)|Core Resilience Analysis (2.1) applied to an existing asset|
|**Level 3 — Forensic Engineering**|Invasive sample testing, real-time stress monitoring, dynamic wind/water simulation for high-risk assets|JFS's existing Expert Witness and Forensic Engineering service — already on the service list, and a strong ready-made anchor for this tier|

Outcome: **assurance-ready engineering reports** — evidence an owner can hand to an insurer, a lender, or an ESG advisor to support a coverage, covenant, or disclosure position, not just an internal capex-planning document. Post-occupancy reconciliation (comparing modelled vs. measured outcomes once works proceed) remains a Year 3 recurring-revenue add-on beyond the three tiers.

**Worth being disciplined about, not just adopting the framing wholesale**: "this asset may become uninsurable" is a genuinely sharper commercial hook than a compliance pitch, but insurability and covenant compliance are decisions made by insurers and lenders, not by JFS. The appointment and reporting language in Section 7 needs to hold that line — JFS provides assurance-ready evidence, not an insurability or covenant guarantee.

This maps directly onto JFS's existing **Remediation & Diagnostics** service line (Façade Audits, Water Ingress Investigation and Testing, Existing Asset Material Life Forecasting, Expert Witness and Forensic Engineering) — arguably a stronger existing foothold than the new-build lead-in triad has.

Put together, 2.2 and 2.3 give JFS a lead-in mechanism at both ends of the building lifecycle: new-build via Façade Consulting/Building Physics, existing-building via Remediation & Diagnostics.

### 2.4 Explicitly Out of Scope for This Positioning

Daylight/glare/solar reflection (occupant-comfort framing), acoustics, and non-thermal seismic/structural movement are real, valid services — but only loosely coupled to climate hazard exposure. Keeping them out of the resilience narrative, while still offering them opportunistically (client-requested, partnered, or bundled), protects the focus of the three tiers above.

## 5. Delivery Model

### 5.1 Lean by design

The group should stay lean deliberately. Most of Year 1 can run through existing JFS staff being redirected or trained rather than new hires. Two targeted additions are worth considering — not a team build:

- One mid-level analyst/modeller to absorb JV3 lead-in volume once proven, so senior capacity isn't consumed by compliance-volume delivery instead of higher-value resilience work.
- Specialist partner or subcontractor relationships (not FTEs) for dynamic hygrothermal (WUFI) modelling and physical wind-tunnel test commissioning — used on a subset of projects, so pay for access rather than headcount. Check internally for WUFI capability first before assuming an external partner is needed.

### 5.2 The data pipeline

The concrete answer to how the group scales physics-based analysis without scaling headcount: take architectural PDFs, send them to an offshore model-build team with a clear brief, get back an analytical model, enrich it locally with NCC compliance and HVAC data, feed it into downstream façade workflows.

- **Platform**: pilot **Better Building** as the primary platform — browser-based, purpose-built Australian NCC/J1V3 workflows, clean EnergyPlus epJSON export. Run **DesignBuilder** in parallel for cases needing deeper bidirectional editing.
- **Pipeline**: PDF intake → automated sheet preparation → offshore analytical modelling → structured export (IDF/epJSON/dsbXML) → canonical model with provenance → automated QA → NCC/HVAC enrichment → EnergyPlus compliance model → façade data products.
- **The governance boundary that matters most**: offshore builds _observed geometry only_ — zones, surfaces, openings, shading, traceable to source drawings — never engineering judgement. NCC interpretation, thermal-bridge methodology, façade U-value/SHGC acceptance, and sign-off stay local. This is what keeps one local analyst able to credibly review rather than re-derive.
- **Four canonical layers**, kept separate so an engineering-assumption change never forces a geometry rebuild: Source, Geometry, Engineering, Compliance.
- **Handoff discipline**: never accept just a native file back from offshore. Require a structured submission bundle (source drawings, exchange formats, assumptions log, QA results) with a manifest and stable object IDs traceable to source drawings.
- **Where to spend development effort**: automated QA, not a geometry editor. Closed-volume checks, matching adjacent surfaces, no incorrectly-adiabatic walls, floor-area reconciliation — this is the actual moat, since nominally valid exchange files routinely contain broken geometry that only structured validation catches.
- **Pilot plan**: run one Class 1 dwelling and one small Class 5–9 building through both platforms in parallel, measuring brief-prep time, modelling time, QA failure rate, and local correction time. Optimise for total local review time, not raw offshore modelling speed — that's the real cost driver in a lean model.

## 6. Roadmap

### 0–6 months

1. **Climate-risk register add-on** to existing façade scopes — minimal new capability, mostly repackaging what the team already assesses informally.
2. **Target the two channels with real current pull-through**: a named-account plan for GPT/111 Eagle Street, and a structured push into the government-procured cohort, positioned as protecting existing rating commitments rather than a new ASRS pitch.
3. **Name and package the service line** — a one-page capability statement, even before the technical build-out completes.
4. **Remedial-works reframe**: shift from "what failed, how do we fix it" to "what failed, why, and will the fix hold under the future hazard profile" — a natural extension of work already being quoted.
5. **Confirm and upgrade the Core three** (2.1, 4) — the firm-level foundation already exists; the work is methodology depth, not a build from scratch.
6. **Build and launch the Pre-Planning Advisory Brief** (2.2) — a near-zero-cost, automated massing/glazing/budget brief for architects, given before an ESD consultant is appointed. Low production cost once the archetype database and parametric screen exist; the main upfront work is building that automation, styling it to JFS's brand standards, and locking the liability framing (Section 7) before the first one goes out. JV3/daylight/materials-index capability (already largely present per Section 4) gets resolved downstream through this relationship rather than sold as a standalone lead-in.
7. **Launch the Level 1 Desktop Screening product and initiate 2–3 channel-partner relationships** across both triggers (2.3) — ESG consultancies, sustainability advisors, and independent NABERS assessors on the transition-risk side; and a first conversation with an insurer, institutional lender, or Big 4 climate-advisory practice on the physical-risk side. Sequence the PI/liability setup (Section 7) before this goes to market.

- Deepen the channel-partner relationships from Quick Win 7 into a repeatable referral flow.
- Data pipeline MVP: the Better Building vs. DesignBuilder platform pilot (5.2).

- Target Group 2 entities (from July 2026) as a defined sales campaign using Year 1 case studies.
- Build out full Level 2/Level 3 delivery capability for the Building Enclosure Resilience Audit (2.3) beyond the Level 1 screening product, and begin two distinct benchmark assets: a **project-experience database** (JFS's own delivered work — typology, climate zone, intervention type, capex/m², embodied carbon/m²) and a **market-intelligence register** (what major asset owners disclose about facade climate risk versus what engineering standard actually backs it — the Section 3 disclosure review is the seed for this). The first is a delivery moat; the second is a standing BD tool for named-account outreach — both more durable than CRREM familiarity alone, which any competitor can acquire.

### Year 3

- Public positioning as the tech-enabled, mid-market alternative to the multidisciplinary giants.
- Structured outreach ahead of the Group 3 wave (July 2027) into newly in-scope mid-size businesses.
- Evaluate productising the internal pipeline/tooling as a portal or reporting product — an additional revenue line beyond consulting hours.
- Productise an **Independent Building Enclosure Assurance Review** (a second opinion on a Tier-1 firm's retrofit strategy, or a standalone Level 2/3 engagement for an insurer or lender) and **post-occupancy reconciliation** as recurring revenue.
- Use the accumulated project evidence base as the next moat, as today's differentiators commoditise.

## 7. Governance & Risk

- **Methodology and QA standards** for climate-risk assessment and physics-based analysis — needed for consistency now and audit-defensibility later, and don't exist as JFS assets yet.
- **Evidence-retention and disclosure-support templates** (hazard screens, vulnerability assessments, metrics frameworks) — build once, reprice and resell across clients.
- **Professional indemnity and liability**, specifically for the Building Enclosure Resilience Audit (2.3) and ASRS-linked work generally: the risk is a planning-level output (a CRREM stranding date, an ASRS metric, an insurer's risk flag) being treated as an implied guarantee of a physical-project outcome — or, specifically for the insurance/covenant-driven trigger, being read as a guarantee of insurability or covenant compliance, which are decisions made by insurers and lenders, not by JFS.
    - Confirm PI insurance scope with JFS's broker for remedial/retrofit advisory work: water-ingress/condensation/thermal-performance claims, cladding exclusions, design-and-construct exclusions.
    - Appointment language stating energy/carbon/NABERS/CRREM/valuation/insurability/covenant outcomes are modelling estimates or engineering evidence, not guarantees; named reliance parties only; written sign-off required for contractor substitutions.
    - Scope discipline: resist a definitive retrofit recommendation from a CRREM stranding date or an insurer's risk flag alone — run the Level 1 routine-vs-transition-risk diagnostic (2.3) first. A planning signal isn't an engineering scope, and a genuine transition-risk finding is a different, more urgent conversation than a routine-refurbishment one.
    - The Pre-Planning Advisory Brief (2.2) needs the same discipline, stated on the document itself: it's non-statutory directional design advice that "does not replace statutory JV3" or the ESD consultant's compliance role — the massing bounds and glazing guidance are a design starting point, not a substitute for the statutory pathway that follows.
    - This is a one-off setup cost — sequence into the first 90 days, before the first partner-referred engagement.
